The Season That Moved

Thirty-four years at the weir, read line by line, like the almanac the valley used to keep.

The valley’s rainy season now begins about four weeks later than it did in the early 1990s, and when it comes, it lands harder. In the first five recorded years, 1990–1994, the first sustained rain reached the weir around September 9 (day 252 of the year); across the last five verified years — 2018 and 2020–2023 — it held off until around October 7 (day 280), a shift of 28 days. The seasons themselves grew heavier: about 606 mm then, about 692 mm now — a rise of 86 mm, or 14% — and the wettest single day of a season climbed from about 43 mm to about 72 mm.

Read with care: the instrument changed after 1996 — a manual gauge until then, the automated weir station from 1997 — so comparisons across that line carry the change with them. Three years are missing outright, one 2019 reading remains unverified, and this page describes the shift in one valley’s record, not its cause.

The record, year by year

How to read each line: the dot places the year’s first sustained rain on a fixed September–October shelf (the shelf runs from Sep 1, day 244, to Oct 15, day 288 — marks at Sep 15 and Oct 1 fall at days 258 and 274). The bar draws the season’s total rainfall on a fixed scale from 550 mm to 750 mm, and the red figure is the season’s heaviest single day. Missing years stay on the page as hatched gaps.

About this record

What counts as the season’s start. The weir’s working definition: the first day of the water year (September 1 is day 244) that opens the first 10-day window with at least 25 mm of rain in total. The season’s total runs from that onset through the last qualifying 10-day window, and the heaviest day is the largest 24-hour rainfall inside the season.

The instrument changed. From 1990 through 1996 the readings came from a manual gauge, read twice daily. From 1997 the automated weir station took over. Every comparison this page makes between the early years and the recent ones crosses that boundary, and should be read with it in mind.

Three years are missing. 1998 was lost to a gauge failure that kept the station offline all season; 2003 and 2004 went unrecorded when monitoring funding lapsed. They are drawn as gaps, and no average on this page reaches across them as if they were there.

One reading is unverified. The 132 mm heaviest-day figure of 2019 came from a sensor spike during the Oct 12 storm and has not been corroborated by neighboring stations. It appears in the record with that label, and 2019 is left out of the recent-era averages above.

What this page can and cannot say. This record describes one valley’s weir. It contains no attribution analysis: the page describes the shift, not its cause.
