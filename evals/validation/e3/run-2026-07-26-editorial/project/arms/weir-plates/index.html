The Season That Moved

Three plates from the town weir’s ledger, drawn plainly, with the reporter’s notes written where they belong: on the record itself.

Thirty-four years of measurements at the town weir show the valley’s wet season arriving about four weeks later than it did in the early 1990s, and arriving heavier. Across 1990–1994 the first sustained rain came around September 9 — day 252 of the year. Across the last five verified years, 2018 and 2020–2023, it held off until around October 7 — day 280 — a drift of 28 days. Season totals rose from about 606 mm to about 692 mm (up 86 mm, or 14%), and the season’s heaviest single day rose from about 43 mm to about 72 mm.

The record keeps its own caveats in view: the instrument changed after 1996 (manual gauge, then automated weir), three seasons are missing outright, one 2019 reading is unverified — and this page describes the shift in one valley’s record, not its cause.

Plate I — When the rain comes

The weir has kept the valley’s time for a generation. What its ledger shows is not a dramatic year here or there but a patient drift: the season that used to open before the autumn fair now waits for the second week of October.

“Cut the meadow the last week of August; rain never came before the fair.” — weir keeper’s log, 1994 (fictional)

A later, heavier season is not an abstraction here. It is the lane under water in October, and the parish answering in its own practical way.

About this record

What counts as the season’s start. The weir’s working definition: the first day of the water year (September 1 is day 244) that opens the first 10-day window with at least 25 mm of rain in total. The season’s total runs from that onset through the last qualifying 10-day window; the heaviest day is the largest 24-hour rainfall inside the season.

The instrument changed. 1990–1996: a manual gauge, read twice daily. 1997 onward: the automated weir station. Every early-vs-recent comparison on this page crosses that boundary and is marked with the seam where it does.

Three years are missing; one reading is unverified. 1998 (gauge failure), 2003–2004 (funding lapsed) are drawn as open gaps and excluded from every average. 2019’s 132 mm heaviest day is unverified — a sensor spike not yet corroborated — so 2019 is drawn hollow and left out of the recent-era averages.

What this page can and cannot say. This record describes one valley’s weir. It contains no attribution analysis: the page describes the shift, not its cause.
